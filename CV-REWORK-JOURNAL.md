# CV rework — journal

Raw material for a write-up. What was wrong, what changed, and _why_ — kept in
the order the problems were actually found, because several of them only became
visible once an earlier one was fixed.

> Section de tête rédigée en français (matière première pour l'article FR) ; le
> reste du journal est en anglais.

## Point de départ : le CV comme problème d'ingénierie

Origine, honnête : le CV précédent était fait sous **Sketch**. Un outil de
design — donc un CV pensé **pour l'œil**, pas pour une machine. Deux problèmes,
tous les deux structurels :

- **L'export PDF était lourd.** Assez pour buter sur la limite d'upload la plus
  courante des formulaires de candidature et des ATS : **2 Mo**. Un CV qu'on ne
  peut pas envoyer ne sert à rien, quel que soit son contenu.
- **Je n'y touchais jamais.** Toujours en mission, occupé — pas un argument de
  vente terrible, mais c'est la réalité : un CV qu'on ne maintient pas vieillit
  tout seul, et refaire une maquette Sketch pour un chiffre qui bouge est assez
  pénible pour qu'on ne le fasse pas.

Le contournement que j'avais trouvé pour repasser sous les 2 Mo était le pire
possible : **exporter une version « allégée » = une image aplatie.** Léger, oui,
mais une _vulgaire image_ — aucune couche texte, presque aucune info
exploitable. Pour un humain qui l'ouvre, ça passe. Pour tout le reste, c'est un
mur blanc.

Or c'est précisément là que le contexte a changé : **un CV est aujourd'hui lu
d'abord par des machines** — ATS qui parsent, LLM qui résument et notent,
moteurs qui indexent. Le recruteur humain ne voit souvent le dossier qu'_après_
ce premier filtre. D'où la décision qui cadre tout le reste :

> **Bosser le CV pour les machines d'abord.**

Formulé en ingénieur : le PDF doit être **léger ET lisible par une machine en
même temps** — pas l'un au prix de l'autre. L'ancien monde imposait un choix
(image = légère mais aveugle · PDF Sketch = riche mais trop lourd). Tenir les
deux bouts à la fois est tout le sujet technique de ce qui suit.

Un mot de motivation, parce qu'il explique le niveau de détail du reste : je
suis un bourrin. Plutôt que bricoler un CV vite fait, j'en ai fait un **terrain
d'apprentissage et d'expérimentation** — le genre de chantier qu'on ne peut pas
toujours se permettre en mission. Le pipeline, les dérivations, les données
structurées : c'est un side-project autant qu'un CV.

### L'arc, en trois états

| État                             | Poids                              | Couche texte / lisibilité machine        |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| PDF Sketch (l'original)          | trop lourd (> 2 Mo, upload refusé) | riche à l'œil, mais impossible à envoyer |
| Image aplatie (le contournement) | léger                              | **aucune** — vulgaire image, ~0 info     |
| Page `/cv` → PDF (aujourd'hui)   | **680 Ko**                         | couche texte exacte + `/Info` + JSON-LD  |

Les deux premiers états échouent pour la même raison profonde, à deux endroits
différents : ils sont pensés pour l'œil. Le troisième **part de la machine** et
retombe sur ses pieds côté humain (§8). Tout le reste du journal — la couche
texte détruite (§0), qpdf plutôt que Ghostscript (§11), les gradients qui
survivent à l'extraction (§9), le `/Info` et le JSON-LD (§6) — n'est que la mise
en œuvre de cette bascule : _le poids et la lisibilité machine cessent d'être un
compromis._

## 0. The finding that reframes everything

The published PDF had a **destroyed text layer for months**, and nothing showed
it. The page rendered perfectly. Only extraction was broken:

```
Simpler systems        →  er systems
Contributing to open…  →  ibuting to open…
As CTO at Hove         →  TO at Hove
written on moox.io     →  i en on moox.io
TypeScript · JavaScript →  T eSc i · Ja aSc i  /  yp  /  r pt
```

The cause: `gs -sDEVICE=pdfwrite`, run as an image-compression step. Ghostscript
does not compress a PDF — it **rewrites the whole document**, text included, and
reorders/drops glyph-show operators.

Why this matters beyond the bug: three LLMs (Gemini, ChatGPT, Claude) were asked
to review the CV. Gemini reported "critical typos" — `hello@moox.lo`,
`mocx.lo` — that **do not exist in the document**. It was reading the corruption.
Claude reported `Å French Native`, `PUTA INDECODE. IO`, `in my teamsfor`,
`atop React expert` — same thing.

So a meaningful share of the "feedback" was noise generated by a broken file, and
there was no way to know without extracting the text by hand.

**Lesson:** a PDF's text layer is invisible infrastructure. It has no visual
symptom, so it needs an explicit check. `pdftotext out.pdf - | head -40` is now
documented in the export script.

### The fix, measured

| Post-processing          | Size       | Text layer |
| ------------------------ | ---------- | ---------- |
| Ghostscript (`/printer`) | 431 KB     | **broken** |
| none                     | 991 KB     | intact     |
| **qpdf**                 | **623 KB** | intact     |

qpdf recompresses streams and images without touching text-showing operators.

Then two more passes on weight, neither of which touches the text:

- **pngquant through request interception.** Chrome embeds bitmaps losslessly,
  so the portrait alone was 242 KB of the PDF. Rather than degrade the source
  asset, the export script intercepts the image request at render time
  (`page.route`) and serves a quantized buffer. `public/max-1.png` is untouched;
  the site keeps its full-quality file. The photo is greyscale, so a 256-entry
  palette covers it exactly — 242 KB → **41 KB**, visually free.

Final: **665 KB, text layer exact, 5 more icons than the broken 431 KB version.**

## 1. The positioning problem, and how it got resolved

The CV opened with **Senior Front-End Architect**. His own doubt, in his words:
_"ce n'est peut-être pas bon et pas honnête"_.

The honesty test: if a client hires on that title and watches him work for three
months, do they feel misled? No — he does own the technical decisions, alone or
leading. So it was not a lying problem. It was an **effectiveness** problem:

- Clients post _"Développeur React Native"_, _"Lead Front-End"_. "Architecte
  Front-End" is rarer and skews large-corp/consultancy, shrinking his match
  surface — while he needed a mission fast.
- "Architect" also signals _expensive, draws diagrams, does not code_ — the
  opposite of what he sells.

But the deeper issue was that **no job title on the CV supported the claim**:

| Mission  | Was                    | Became                   |
| -------- | ---------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Aardvark | Web & Mobile Developer | Lead Front-End Developer |
| Exem     | Front-End Developer    | Lead Front-End Developer |
| Hove     | Mobile & Web Developer | Lead Front-End Developer |

**The unlock:** stop claiming architecture as a _title_ (contestable, and he
felt it) and claim it as a _behaviour_ (demonstrable). Fix the per-mission
titles, and the headline stops being a promotion — it becomes a summary of what
is written underneath.

Final headline: `Lead Front-End Developer.` with the subtitle
`React, React Native & cross-platform.` — the platform reach in the terms
clients actually search for ("React Native" beats "Web & Mobile"), and the
keyword-dense long form kept in `<title>`/`<meta>` where length is free.

### The contradiction this created, and its fix

Two rounds later, an LLM review caught what the change had introduced: three
entries titled **Lead Front-End Developer**, with **"Solo front-end dev"** in
bold violet directly underneath. _Lead of whom?_

The culprit was never the title — it was the word **Solo**. Replaced by
`End-to-end front-end ownership`. Ownership is the claim; headcount is not.

Note two LLMs reached **opposite** conclusions here: ChatGPT said "Lead"
undersells him (suggested Staff/Principal/Architect); Claude said it oversells
given he works alone. When reviewers contradict each other, the shared signal is
not "use title X" — it is _the title does not match the evidence_.

## 2. Numbers: one vindicated, one wrong, one overstated

**The 200M was mine to be wrong about.** I flagged "200M+ downloads/month on
open source I created" as a credibility risk, assuming it leaned on stylelint
(where he is explicitly labelled _instigator_, not creator). `STATS.md` settled
it: the figure comes from packages whose **first npm publisher** is `moox` —
postcss-calc (75.7M), postcss-custom-properties (33.9M),
postcss-color-rebeccapurple, postcss-color-hex-alpha, postcss-font-variant.
He had even _excluded_ packages he only maintains (postcss-selector-parser at
611M/mo, color-name at 1.36B/mo). More rigorous than most CVs.

The real problem was not honesty, it was **legibility**: none of those package
names appeared anywhere. A reader saw 200M, then found cssnext and stylelint
below, and could not reconcile. Naming them turned a claim into a checkable
fact.

**cssnext read as dead.** The card showed `130+ downloads / week` next to
stylelint's `10M+`. The source said `130k+` — the "k" was being dropped at
render. Off by a factor of 1000, in the direction that made his own project look
abandoned.

**The teaching years were overstated — by me.** The entry said "~8 years
lecturing". I propagated it as "Eight years". Counting the actual entries: 2012,
2013, 2014, then 2018, 2019, 2020 — **six academic years across an eight-year
span**, with a 2015–2017 gap. Now `6 of 8 years taught`, and the gap is stated
in the comment rather than smoothed over.

**GitHub stars could not be reconciled.** 12.3K at the top vs 5.3k + 3.2k + 1.2k
visible on page 2. Adding `across 89 repos I own` closes the question in four
words.

**Blog posts were undercounted.** The tile derived 27 from `blog.json`, which
only indexes moox.io. He also wrote **22 articles on putaindecode.io**. 27 read
as thin for two decades; **49** does not.

## 3. Content: from list to story

The formula applied to every key experience: **situation → the hard part → what
I did → the number.** The hard part is what was missing everywhere — without
tension there is no story, only an inventory.

### Hove — the best material was absent

The strongest fact on the entire CV was not on it: **the CTO called him back a
year after he left, to rescue the project.** Third-party validation of
competence, and it was nowhere.

Second strongest: he asked to **shrink** the team. Everyone asks for more people.

Two guardrails applied:

- **Nothing about the predecessor.** His account of the lead dev who replaced him
  was unflattering and probably deserved. A sentence aimed at an identifiable
  predecessor reads as _this person will badmouth colleagues_. The story is
  strong without him — it is his own decision that carries it.
- **"Reduction of the team"**, sitting in the markdown, is ambiguous out of
  context (reads as layoffs). Became _"rebuilt it as a smaller, senior team"_ —
  same fact, framed as the technical choice it was.

Also dropped: the decommissioning and the 2023 budget cut. Neither is his
decision nor his performance, and a CV owes no explanation for why a mission
ends. Useful material for the interview, noise on paper.

### Exem — he was underselling it

His own summary: _"rien de dur, tout était bien conçu"_. What he actually built:
a background map synchronized **both ways** with the whole app, every pan,
filter and selection living in the URL so any view can be shared or restored,
sometimes with several map instances on screen. That is exactly the class of
problem where most implementations desynchronize or loop.

Also recovered from an offhand remark: **nearly every component has a skeleton
state instead of a spinner**, so the UI never flashes empty. Most developers ship
spinners. That detail is a craft signal and it was mentioned in passing.

A fabricated metric got removed on his own initiative: `1,000s map markers` was
invented. The truth — _far more measurement points than a map can draw, so they
cluster with Supercluster_ — is stronger, because it names the difficulty
instead of quantifying it falsely.

### The AI angle — the non-obvious version

What everyone writes: _AI-powered_, _10x productivity_, _prompt engineering_,
Claude listed as a skill next to TypeScript.

His actual insight, offered casually: _"ma précision dans les prompts me fait
gagner du temps, et j'ai rarement des bugs, contrairement à d'autres devs qui se
plaignent."_

Translated: **the bottleneck is not the model, it is the clarity of the
intent.** That is a seniority claim disguised as an AI claim — and a junior
cannot copy it, unlike "I master Claude Code".

Rendered as a skills card, not a logo:

> **AI-assisted engineering** — Claude Code, daily · Precise intent in, working
> code out · Strict types, e2e tests & CI as the safety net

The guardrails are the differentiator, and they are already proven elsewhere on
the CV (Playwright in CI at Aardvark, Detox + Jenkins at Hove).

## 4. Testimonials: the compliment/proof inversion

He had extracted excerpts from eight LinkedIn recommendations. In **five out of
six** used, he had kept the sentence praising him _as a person_ and cut the one
that **proved something**.

| Kept                                                  | Cut                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "one of those really rare engineers… takes ownership" | _"As a scrappy startup where absolutely every dollar mattered, we were constantly looking for ways to avoid hiring contractors — we instead found ourselves consistently looking for reasons to hang on to Max."_ |
| "truly a top React expert"                            | _"j'ai eu la chance d'avoir Maxime dans mes équipes **pendant 3 ans au moins**"_                                                                                                                                  |
| "loves sharing his thirst for learning"               | _"toujours disponible et pédagogue, ce qui m'a permis d'appréhender React Native très rapidement"_                                                                                                                |
| "knowledgeable and motivated frontend developer"      | _"not only does he stay up-to-date with best practices: **he actively contributes to them** … from line-by-line code reviews to architectural discussions"_                                                       |
| "a passionate and ambitious professional"             | _"une réelle compréhension des besoins business"_ + _"même à distance (remote worker @shopbot)"_ — remote work attested by a manager **in 2012**                                                                  |

The startup-retention paragraph is probably the single best sentence in the
whole dossier: it is **economic proof**, not adjectives, from the person who
signed the invoices, and it answers the only real objection to an expensive
freelance. Nobody invents a detail like _"every dollar mattered"_.

**The rule that falls out:** keep the sentence a competitor could not also have
written about them. "Passionate", "motivated", "rare engineer", "human
qualities" — anyone can have those written about them. "We kept looking for
reasons to hang on to him", "three years in my teams", "got me productive on
React Native", "from line-by-line reviews to architectural discussions", "even
remotely, in 2012" — those are facts.

Attribution was also fixed: first names only ("Aissa", "Alex") became full names
plus the credential that makes the quote land — `Software Engineer at Hove`,
`Software Engineer · stylelint maintainer` — with a link to the LinkedIn
recommendations page so the whole block is checkable.

## 5. Data model: three hacks removed by one field

The CV layer had accumulated manual overrides to fake a merge of several
markdown entries into one row: `period` (a hand-written date span) and
`description_long` (a name that lied — it was never "the description but
longer", it was the CV pitch covering a whole group).

Replaced by **`cvGroup`**:

```yaml
cvGroup: hove # on every mission of the group
highlight: true # on the one carrying the copy, icon and stats
```

The row's date span is now **derived** from the group (`min(dateStart)` →
`max(dateEnd)`), which is what a CV should show: a CV lists a **client**, not
contracts. His markdown was right all along — Hove is genuinely three missions,
FKLG genuinely two — it was the CV layer that had to fold them.

It reproduces exactly the spans he had hand-written (Hove 2017–2023, FKLG
2023–2026, IUT 2012–2020), and it removed **three hacks** in one move:

- an `isTeaching()` regex guessing teaching entries from title keywords
- a hard-coded `normalizeCompany()` for the Kisio Digital → Hove rebrand
- a per-company dedup added two rounds earlier

Then `cvGroupPeriods()` went one step further: **under 11 months between two
missions is an ordinary contract break; a year or more is a real interruption
worth showing.** Hove displays `2017 – 2018 · 2019 – 2023`, which finally agrees
with the pitch's _"called back a year later"_. IUT still shows `2012 – 2020`
because its summer gaps are three months. Derived, not authored.

Other model fixes in the same spirit — every one of them replacing a positional
hack with a named intention:

| Hack                                                                  | Replaced by                                                     |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `slice(0, 1)` / `slice(1)` on skill cards, subtitle hard-coded in JSX | `feature` + `subtitle` in the data                              |
| `testimonials.slice(0, 3)` for the CV                                 | `cv: true` flag, so site order and CV selection are independent |
| Pékin Express and a student PalmOS project listed as jobs             | `personal: true`                                                |
| `description_long`                                                    | `cvPitch`                                                       |
| JSX-baked bold in quotes                                              | `**bold**` markers + `{ en, fr }` + `originalLang`              |

### Two rendering bugs found along the way

- **Hashtags are truncated to 6** — so `#JavaScript #React` were rendering and
  `#Detox #Jenkins #Playwright #Fastlane` were being cut. The distinctive tags
  now lead, with a comment in each frontmatter explaining why order matters.
- **`label.toLowerCase()` was destroying acronyms**: `1 day → 1 click qa &
release`, `React Native api`. Replaced by `lowerKeepingAcronyms()`.

## 6. Machine-readability, deliberately last

Once the text layer was correct, the remaining gap was structured data.

- **PDF `/Info`** — Chrome only writes `/Title`, so `/Author` was **empty**. A
  parser trusting metadata over layout had no name at all, or took the first
  line of the page ("Lead Front-End Developer.") for one. Now filled via qpdf's
  JSON round-trip.
- **JSON-LD `Person`** on `/cv` — removes every guess about which string is the
  name, the title, an employer. `worksFor` deduplicates by `cvGroup` exactly
  like the rendered rows, so the structured data can never list a mission the
  page folded away. `name` + `alternateName` settles the "Max. / Maxime
  Thirouin" ambiguity without touching the design.
- **Single source of truth.** The export script is plain Node and cannot import
  a TSX module, so the strings travel through the rendered `<head>`:
  `consts.tsx → cv.tsx head() → <meta> → page.evaluate() → PDF /Info`.

A finding surfaced while wiring this up: the three pages of the site advertise
**three different job titles** (Architect / Architect & Developer / Lead
Developer). Publishing `Person` JSON-LD on all three would state that
contradiction in machine-readable form — worse than no structured data. Parked
in `CV-TO-WEB.md` until the titles are aligned.

## 8. From spec to page: the CV stopped being a Word-shaped thing

Up to here the work was content and pipeline on the _old_ `/resume` render. `CV-SPEC.md`
is the seed of the next move — a text-only skeleton of a dedicated `/cv` page. It
is worth keeping precisely because it is _pre-decision_: it still headlines
**Senior Front-End Architect**, still asks "keep age (40)?", still lists "They
tried to hire me". Everything §1 and §7 later resolved is visible in it as an
open question. The spec is the before; the built page is the after.

The build made one decision that shaped all the others: **the CV is not a
separate document, it is a route.** `/cv` (`cv.tsx`, ~1050 lines) renders with
the site's own design system — `GradientLinear`, the `textFlashy1…4` theme
tokens, `GradientText`, the same pills and stat primitives — and Chrome's
`page.pdf` turns that page into the PDF. No CV stylesheet, no separate template
to drift.

What falls out of "the page _is_ the document":

- **A white/gradient band rhythm** carries straight over from the site: white
  header → gradient summary band → white stats → gradient skills mosaic → white
  experiences → gradient Open Source band → white proof/education. The site
  already looked like this; the CV inherits it for free.
- **`zoom: 0.75` + `@page { size: A4; margin: 0 }`** let the page reuse the
  theme's sizing scale at print scale instead of re-picking every number for
  paper (_"pdf zoom to avoid having to resize everything… keep using theme sizes
  easily"_, `cv.tsx:278`).
- **A `position: fixed` border frame** — because a fixed element repeats on every
  printed page in Chrome, one div draws the gradient frame around _both_ PDF
  pages (`cv.tsx:315`).
- **Forced light mode** on mount, restored on unmount: the CV always prints
  light regardless of the visitor's theme (`cv.tsx:248`).

The componentization is small and named for what it does: `CvSectionTitle`
(gradient heading + hairline rule), `CvStatTile` (the "player-card" tile, three
variants — `plain` on light bands, `gradient` for the one figure that must pop,
`glass` inside dark sections — and one layout that renders both stat shapes),
`CvSkillCard` (dark gradient card with the tech logo as a 9%-opacity watermark),
`CvOpenSourceCard` (one band, GitHub watermark, Arctic Vault badge, the four
entries on a 2×2 glass grid). None of them is CV-specific styling; each is the
site's vocabulary pointed at print.

### The page as single source of truth

The sharpest consequence is machine-readability with **no duplicated strings**.
`cvMetaKeywords` and friends are authored once in `consts.tsx` and consumed
three times — the HTML `<head>`, the JSON-LD `Person`, and the PDF's `/Info`
dictionary — because the export script _reads the `<meta>` tags back off the
rendered page_ rather than importing the TSX (`cv.tsx:99`, `consts.tsx:347`).
A plain Node script that cannot import a component still can't disagree with it.
`cvJsonLd` is _"the machine-readable twin of the page… built from the rendered
data, so it cannot drift from what a human reads"_ (`cv.tsx:284`), and its
`worksFor` list is deduped by `cvGroup` **exactly like the rendered rows**, so
the structured data can never advertise a mission the page folded away.

## 9. `GradientText`: the same invisible-infrastructure trap, avoided this time

§0 was a text layer destroyed with no visual symptom. The gradient headings are
the same class of trap, caught before it shipped. Three ways to render gradient
text, two of them silently broken in exactly the medium that matters:

| Technique                        | Fails where                                                                    |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `background-clip: text`          | blank in Safari print / macOS Preview — i.e. the app most people open a PDF in |
| SVG `<text>`                     | unreliable in Chrome's `page.pdf`                                              |
| **per-character solid `<Text>`** | renders in every engine, wraps naturally, **stays selectable and extractable** |

`GradientText` takes the third road: it interpolates a solid hex per character
along the gradient (`tinycolor.mix`) and emits each glyph as its own colored
`<Text>`. The gradient is quantized per character rather than continuous within a
glyph — _imperceptible for headings_ (`GradientText.tsx:25`). Every gradient
heading, the `Max.` wordmark, and every section title go through it. Area
gradients (bands, cards, the shared `#cvGrad` icon fill) stay real vector fills
via `GradientLinear` / SVG `LinearGradient`, which print cleanly.

**Lesson, restated:** anything decorative that lands in the PDF has to be proven
against _extraction and Preview_, not against how it looks in Chrome. The pretty
gradient that vanishes in macOS Preview is the same failure mode as the text
layer — no symptom until someone else opens the file.

## 10. The data model, completed: stats became data, figures became derivations

§5 removed the hand-written `period`. This pass finishes the job: the last
hand-typed things on the CV — the per-mission numbers — became typed data, and
the global figures became computations.

**`ResumeStat` (`api.tsx:8`)** — `{ stat, label, title?, comment?, url? }`. Every
figure is now a frontmatter object with an optional **proof `url`** (npm, GitHub).
"Numbers must be checkable" stopped being a rule you follow by hand and became a
field. `CvStat = ResumeStat & { highlight? }` marks the one tile rendered as a
filled gradient.

**The flag vocabulary** that drives selection, all named intentions, no
positions: `highlight` (the entry carrying a group's copy/icon/stats),
`education`, `openSource`, `personal` (kept in the data, never shown as a job —
a parser would file Pékin Express as employment), `job_title`, `icon`,
`cvGroup`, `cvPitch`, `stats`.

**The four profile tiles, sorted by how they're produced:**

| Tile                                        | Source                                                                                                                                                     |
| ------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `19 Years`                                  | **derived** — `thisYear − cvProSince(2007)`                                                                                                                |
| `20+ Projects`                              | **derived** — `roundedDownCount` of freelance-era, non-OSS, non-education entries (same definition as STATS.md §2)                                         |
| `15+ Clients`                               | **derived** — distinct `company` over the same set                                                                                                         |
| `200M+ Downloads/mo` · `12.3K GitHub stars` | **authored baselines** — can't be computed in a static build with no authenticated npm/GitHub call; dated `2026-07-30`, refresh commands in STATS.md §4/§5 |

`roundedDownCount` **floors** to the nearest 5 then adds `+`, _so a figure never
overstates_ (`consts.tsx:453`) — "20+" means 20–24, never a rounded-up 25.
`compactCount` does `12338 → 12.3K`. `lowerKeepingAcronyms` lowercases stat
labels into a running line while sparing acronyms, because _"1 day → 1 click qa &
release" reads like a typo_.

**Two derivations earn their own thresholds, each documented against a failure
it prevents:**

- **`cvMonthRange`** shows months only under `monthPrecisionMaxYears = 3`; longer
  spans fall back to years. Reason: _"2025 – 2026" reads as "finished in January"
  and silently ages the whole CV_ — a recent short mission needs `Sep 2025 – Apr
2026`, a seven-year one doesn't.
- **`cvGroupPeriods`** splits a client's tenure into stints when the gap reaches
  `contiguousGapMonths = 11`: under a year is an ordinary contract break, a year
  or more is a real interruption. Hove renders `2017 – 2018 · 2019 – 2023`, which
  is what the pitch's _"called back a year later"_ describes; a single span would
  contradict its own story.

**Open source became four real entries**, not prose: `cssnext`, `stylelint`,
`phenomic`, `rescript-react-native`, each `openSource: true` with a `stats[]`
array and proof links — the §2 insight ("name the packages") turned into data the
Open Source band renders directly. Titles are exact and unshowy: stylelint is
`Originator` (issue #1), not a claim to have built it.

## 11. The pipeline, second pass: same ban, new render target

§0 is the Ghostscript story and still holds. This commit rebuilt the script
around it:

- **Render target moved from `/resume?pdf=true` to `/cv`.** The PDF is now the
  new page. _(Caveat found in passing: the script's top-of-file header comment
  still describes the old `/resume` flow — stale, code is right, comment isn't.)_
- **pngquant generalized.** The portrait hack from §0 became a rule: `page.route`
  intercepts every PNG ≥ 100 KB and serves a quantized buffer on the wire; source
  files on disk are never touched; sub-100 KB icons pass through untouched.
- **`/Info` via a qpdf JSON round-trip** — export to JSON, patch
  `/Author /Creator /Subject /Keywords` (the fields Chrome leaves empty), re-import.
  Those strings are the `<meta>` tags scraped off the rendered page, closing the
  single-source-of-truth loop from §8.
- **An `img.decode()` gate before snapshotting** — `networkidle` can fire before
  small icons finish decoding, printing them blank. With a page now full of
  mission and tech icons, this is the icon-equivalent of the text-layer check:
  invisible if you don't look.
- Default page margin `10mm → 0mm` (the band rhythm goes edge to edge).

Size went **665 KB → 680 KB**: a denser page with more icons, partly offset by
quantization — text layer exact, `/Info` filled, still two pages. Not a
regression, but the number moved, so it's recorded here rather than left implying
665 is current.

## 12. Translation: what a second language reveals

The site went bilingual (EN default, `/fr` prefix) in one pass. What is worth
recording is not the plumbing — it is everything translation _found_.

**A translated body drifts exactly like a hand-written number.** 25 of the 30
résumé entries already carried a French half, written years ago. Every one of
them translated the _pre-rework_ text: the French for Aardvark was 35 words
against 175 in English, Exem 60 against 189. Nobody had lied; the English was
rewritten (§3) and its translation silently stayed behind. Same failure as the
hand-authored date spans of §10, in a place where derivation is impossible —
so what replaced derivation was a **detector**: the FR/English word-count ratio
per file. French normally runs 1.1–1.3× English; anything at 0.2 was stale, and
anything at 2.7 meant the English had been _cut_ and the French not. That
sorted 30 files into "rewrite" and "keep" in one command, with no judgement
call.

**Translation is a debt scanner.** Walking every user-visible string to
translate it surfaced what reading the same files a dozen times had not: the
home's app card still advertised **"Senior Front-end Architect"**, the exact
title §1 retired everywhere else — the one string that had escaped the pass
because it was hardcoded in a component rather than read from `profile.tsx`. It
now derives from `jobTitle`. The old French copy also carried its own
accumulated typos ("un architecture", "skelette", "dévelopment"), fixed on the
way.

**The language boundary is the loader, not the component.** The obvious design
— every component asks for its language — would have touched every rendering
file. Instead the résumé is resolved once, in the route loaders
(`fetchResume(lang)`), so `ResumeItem` still holds plain strings and no
rendering code learned that translations exist. Only the editorial strings that
live in TypeScript need `useT()`. One boundary, not fifty.

**A4 is a constraint the second language exposes.** French runs ~15%
longer at equal meaning. Page 1 of the CV overflowed, which pushed the explicit
`breakBefore: "page"` one page down: a **3-page CV**, the one thing this whole
rework refuses. Measured rather than argued — `scale` 0.97 buys the overflow
back, three percent, text still vector. Recorded as a per-language constant
with the page-count check next to it, because the next paragraph of French will
threaten it again.

**What deliberately does not translate.** Figures (`50k+`), proper nouns
(`Next.js`, `Issue #1`), company names, tech hashtags — and the job titles the
French market itself uses in English (`Lead Front-End Developer`,
`Full-Stack Developer`). Roles that are genuinely French _were_ translated
(`University Lecturer` → _Enseignant vacataire_). The English fallback is the
type's default: a value left as a plain string simply is English, so a missing
translation degrades to English instead of rendering nothing.

**And the line that was designed to survive it.** §1 chose "I make front-ends
simple. Simple is the hard part." partly because the second sentence repeats
the noun instead of pronominalizing it, so it would translate word for word.
Two months later: "Je rends les front-ends simples. Le simple, c'est le plus
dur." It did.

## 13. Errata found and fixed

The CV's whole thesis is _checkable_, so the journal owes its own errata — both
of these were caught reviewing this commit and fixed in the same pass:

- **`cssnext`'s first stat** (`5.3k GitHub stars`) pointed its proof `url` at
  **phenomic** — a copy-paste artifact. The number was fine; the link landed on
  the wrong repo. Now points at `MoOx/postcss-cssnext` (cssnext's real repo after
  the rename), consistent with the entry's npm link.
- **`generate-resume-pdf.mjs` header comment** still described the Ghostscript /
  `/resume` pipeline it no longer runs. Rewritten to match the qpdf + pngquant +
  `/Info` flow.

The point isn't that a CV can be error-free; it's that when every claim carries a
checkable source, its own mistakes surface on review instead of shipping.

## Recurring lessons

1. **Invisible infrastructure needs an explicit check.** The PDF looked perfect
   for months. Anything with no visual symptom needs a command in the docs.
2. **Never evaluate on a broken artifact.** Three reviews, and part of the
   feedback was hallucinated from corruption.
3. **When reviewers contradict each other, the signal is the disagreement**, not
   either verdict. Two LLMs said opposite things about the job title; both were
   detecting the same title/evidence mismatch.
4. **Derive, don't author.** Every hand-written value drifted: the period, the
   teaching years, the blog count, the availability date. Everything derived
   from the markdown stayed correct for free.
5. **A claim you make about yourself is worth less than the same claim from
   someone who paid you** — and much less than a number.
6. **The flattering sentence is rarely the useful one.** Keep the one a
   competitor could not also have written.
7. **A title is contestable; a behaviour is demonstrable.** The whole
   architect/lead knot dissolved by moving the claim from the headline to the
   evidence.
8. **The invisible-infrastructure trap has more than one door.** First the text
   layer (§0), then gradient text blank in Preview (§9). Both look perfect in
   Chrome and fail only where someone else opens the file. Anything that reaches
   the PDF gets proven against _extraction and Preview_, not the browser.
9. **Make the page the document.** Rendering the CV as a real route, in the
   site's own design system, killed the drift between "the site", "the PDF" and
   "the structured data" — one source, read three ways (`<head>`, JSON-LD,
   `/Info`). The cheapest way to keep two artifacts in sync is to not have two.
10. **A rule becomes reliable only when it becomes a field.** "Numbers must be
    checkable" held by discipline until `ResumeStat.url` made proof part of the
    data; "never overstate" held until `roundedDownCount` floored instead of
    rounded. Encode the principle where the value lives.
11. **What cannot be derived needs a detector.** A translation can't be computed
    from its source, so it drifts like any hand-written value (§12) — and
    silently, because both versions still read fine on their own. The FR/EN
    word-count ratio found every stale file in one command. When derivation is
    impossible, find the cheap signal that makes drift visible.
12. **Adding a second language is an audit.** It walks every user-visible
    string, so it finds what re-reading the same files never does: a retired job
    title still hardcoded in a component, old typos, and the layout assumptions
    (A4, page breaks) that only hold for the language they were written in.
